There is a page in the handbook arguing that you cannot mandate this tooling,
that you should start with something optional, and that people will form their
own view from evidence if you let them. I believe all of it. I wrote it
carefully and I would defend it.

I have also, in the last few weeks, watched a stuck project move a very long
way with a model doing most of the work, and I have not shown a single
colleague how it was done.

So the page is missing something, and the missing thing is me.

## What the reluctance actually is

It is not that I think they would be wrong to push back. The objections in this
industry are informed, and I have said so in print. People here have watched a
long series of things arrive with confident claims attached and then make the
work worse, and a sceptical reflex is a reasonable thing to have developed.

It is that the evidence I would want to lead with is the thing that makes the
conversation loaded. "This moved a long way and a model did most of it" is
simultaneously the strongest argument available and an announcement that a
category of work someone does might be done differently now. There is no
framing that removes the second half. I have tried several in my head and they
all read as a framing, which is worse.

So I sit on it, and I tell myself it is a timing question, and then some more
time passes.

## Why this is a real failure and not just caution

Because the whole argument for the optional-first approach is that people form
their own view from evidence. If I keep the evidence to myself, there is
nothing for anyone to form a view from, and the approach I have written up as
the right one has not actually been attempted. What has been attempted is me
doing it quietly and hoping the results speak later, which is a different
strategy and a considerably worse one.

It also means the feedback loop is broken in the direction that matters most. I
wrote that the thing I care about is being able to state colleagues'
objections back to them accurately, because that is how you know you are
getting honest information. You cannot get objections to something nobody has
seen. The silence I am currently enjoying is not agreement, it is an absence of
data, and I have been treating those as the same thing.

## What I think the next step is

Not a demo. A demo is a performance with a conclusion built into it, and the
audience knows that.

Something closer to the code reviewer, which worked precisely because it was
easy to ignore and asked nothing of anyone. The equivalent here is probably to
show the failures rather than the win. The stalls, the run that sat waiting for
window focus and looked like progress, the class of bug it cannot see. Leading
with what it costs and where it falls over is both more honest and, I suspect,
more persuasive, because it is not a pitch and the person listening can tell.

Whether I actually do this is the open question, which is an uncomfortable note
to end a post on and an accurate one.
